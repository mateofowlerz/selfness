# My first EAG

EAG changed my life. I can blindly recommend that anyone even slightly interested in safety spend a weekend of their life at EAG.

I have the benefit of having a **slightly** *attractive* CV so it was quite easy to get one-on-ones with really interesting people. To be fair, I also read every single piece of advice online on how to effectively message people.

Because of this, I had 28 one-on-ones over the weekend (!), and I always had a fun plan to do after EAG. I’ll write down how the conference updated my views and some advice I haven’t read anywhere.

First of all, I came into EAG without having strong priors on what I wanted to do, but having an idea and having enough safety context so I could talk about a wide variety of topics at higher abstractions. All my one-on-ones but a couple were at this higher level of abstraction, mostly searching for general career advice and understanding other people's brains. I didn’t need to be a control expert to have interesting conversations with a control researcher, but I had enough so I could talk about it and be *pointed at* next steps.

My goal at EAG was to have stronger views on what my dent in safety has to be. I also wanted to improve my epistemics, and to make friends, as I don’t have any friends who care about this stuff. I achieved all of these.

I built a better intuition of what a safety org looks like and how they think about the future from the inside. I come from the startup world, and it took me a couple of books and iterations to learn the basic startup advice of “do things that don’t scale”, “start small” or “ship fast”. I believe I got a glimpse of what these things are in (at least some) safety orgs.

Lots of them start by doing evals on some particular questions, then plan to do research to help whichever decision makers (labs or governments) implement that research. They have a concrete plan that starts small and grows bigger and bigger as you have more knowledge (due to good evals) and access (due to your good research, which gets noticed). Build awareness, gather information, get your *solutions* implemented where they matter.

There’s also a bitter lesson here which is that a lot of orgs are bottlenecked on getting their solutions implemented into the labs/govs, so stuff that helps achieve that is extremely valuable too, and if you have really low chances of achieving this maybe you should consider changing your idea or trying to get connections inside.

However, I thought people were way more **connected** and had way more **insider info** on the labs, but I *sensed* a lot of work on *reverse-engineering* the lab in order to know what to build. This is, in part, why evals are so important (another thing I didn’t fully understand: why sooo many companies did evals).

I also thought my current abilities didn't transfer so well into safety and that I needed to spend some time doing research on one area until I was actually net positive, and now I don’t think that at all. There’s stuff in control that I feel pretty confident to start helping with soon. Same with threat modeling and red teaming. There are **actually** a lot of things to do, and if you ask what would be helpful, they pretty much tell you. There’s a clear path forward into getting a work trial, if you put in the hours.

Ultimately, I asked a lot: “Tell me how you decided on your theory of change”, and I think I soaked up good epistemics by imitation about how to think about these things. I also asked particular, specific questions on how they do their work. I got some useful tips that I wasn’t expecting.

Overall, it was really fun to talk with these people. It was my first time having so many rich conversations, back to back, and my brain was exhausted by the end of each day (which feels great).

### About starting a company

I think that if I find something no one is working on, or the people working on that are doing it poorly, I’m going to try to build some sort of *“MVP”* and test it against *“the market”*: Twitter, LessWrong and smart people who know about the topic. A big fear of mine is that we could start Melian without knowing shit about anything because we had The Market, and if users slightly like what you’re building you can get going. Non-profits don’t have that, and for-profits usually look like B2Bs who ultimately depend on a couple of big companies and lack a tight feedback loop that a B2C doesn’t.

If this works even a bit, and I see a clear path on what **my** (not the org's) role in the safety agenda can be, I’ll keep trying until I make it happen, just as we did with my previous startup to get it off the ground. I still have a bias towards doing my own thing.

Ultimately, if I can’t think of anything to do, I’ll probably try to join a company I believe in and skip the fellowships. If I can’t do so, I’ll try to join a fellowship. And if this doesn’t work either, I’ll keep trying; if we could take Melian from two random guys in Paraná to users who loved the app, raising money, etc., I can definitely take myself into having impact in a greenfield like safety.

### Recap

Basically what’s so good about EAG is the conversations you have with people. If you enjoyed these conversations a lot, you probably want to keep having them. To do that, you have to be close to these circles and that’s why my bias towards being in the Bay is even higher now. As friends, co-founders, co-workers, thought partners, EA-adjacent people are great. I hope I keep having these types of conversations for a long, long time.
